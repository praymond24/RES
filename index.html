Case study 01
Just Salad · 2024–2025

Salad AI: recommendations for a menu that doesn't exist yet

Role
Director of Product, AI
Team of 7

The problem

A guest opening the app faced a build-your-own menu with more combinations than anyone wants to evaluate while standing in line. New guests defaulted to the first thing they recognized. Regulars ordered the same bowl indefinitely. Both behaviors capped what the digital channel could do.

The interesting constraint: we weren't recommending products. We were recommending recipes that didn't exist yet. A retailer ranks a catalog of SKUs. We had to compose ingredients into something coherent, nutritionally accurate, and actually orderable.

The approach

We captured preference explicitly rather than inferring it. A short survey across three axes — lifestyle and dietary restrictions, nutrition goals, and taste — gave the system a signal to work from on the very first session, before any behavioral history existed.

The generation layer composed four options from that signal, each with a name, a full ingredient list with nutrition, and a dressing pairing. The four were not interchangeable: three sat close to what the guest asked for and one pushed further out, because a recommendation set that only confirms what someone already wants doesn't teach you anything about them.

How we knew it worked

This is the part I'd want to talk about. Generated output is easy to ship and hard to judge. We built the evaluation layer first: a quality rubric, a fixed set of test profiles, named failure categories, and a review process that ran before anything reached a guest. Standardizing hypotheses, evaluation criteria, and launch-readiness checkpoints cut experiment cycle time 30%.

The harder metric was internal. A recommendation engine nobody trusts is shelfware, so I ran demos, company-wide sessions, lunch-and-learns, and implementation playbooks across Marketing, CX, and Operations. Adoption rose 25% and time-to-value dropped 20%.

Case study 02
DroneUp / Walmart · 2021–2024

When the failure mode is a drone over a parking lot

Role
Senior Program Manager

I owned product and roadmap execution for the technology behind Walmart's drone-delivery operations: dispatch, live monitoring, exception handling, and operational readiness across multiple markets. Regulated, safety-critical, and unforgiving of a bad release.

The work was mostly translation. Frontline operators, system incidents, and failure scenarios came in as noise; they had to leave as a prioritized roadmap. Automation and reliability improvements raised completed deliveries 6% and cut human intervention 15%.

It's also where I learned to decide without complete data. You cannot A/B test your way to a rollback plan. That instinct is most of what I brought to shipping AI later, where the output is probabilistic and the honest answer is usually "we don't know yet, so here's the guardrail."

Approach

Three things I believe about shipping AI products

Learned the hard way

  • Start narrow enough to be embarrassing

    One job, one user, one measurable outcome. General-purpose assistants demo well and fail in production because nothing about them can be evaluated. A system with a narrow scope can be told, definitively, that it is wrong.

  • The 70/30 rule

    Do about 70% of the thinking yourself, then let the model challenge, structure, or finish the rest. Reversing that ratio produces output that sounds finished and isn't, and it slowly erodes the judgment you need to catch the difference.

  • Evaluation is a product surface, not a QA step

    Rubrics, golden sets, named failure categories, and shadow runs before a guest ever sees output. Teams that skip this ship faster for one quarter and then spend the next three arguing about whether the thing is working.
